How Each Outlook Window Is Scored

0–6 Hour (Nowcast)

Rolling 6-hour window from now
NWS Watch/Warn + AFD 3.0 HRRR QPF 6-hr 3.0 MRMS Radar QPE 2.0 PWAT 1.5

Built from the products that matter when flooding could begin within hours. Official alerts and the short-fuse HRRR rainfall forecast carry the most weight, radar-observed rainfall shows where heavy rain is already falling, and atmospheric moisture flags whether storms can rain efficiently. Longer-range outlooks are deliberately excluded at this time scale.

6–24 Hour (Day 1)

Rolling window covering the rest of today
HRRR QPF 12-hr 3.0 NWS Watch/Warn + AFD 3.0 PWAT 1.5 ERO 1.0 WPC QPF 1.0 SPC 0.5

Near-term rainfall guidance and official alerts lead, supported by atmospheric moisture and the national excessive-rainfall and storm outlooks. SPC carries the lowest weight because general thunderstorm potential is less directly tied to flooding than rainfall amount, moisture, or flood-specific alerts.

Day 2

Tomorrow, local calendar day
ERO 1.5 NDFD QPF Daily 1.5 SPC 1.0 WPC QPF 1.0

Blends the flash-flood-focused ERO with tomorrow's forecast rainfall from both the local NDFD grids and national WPC guidance, plus thunderstorm potential from SPC. HRRR does not reach reliably this far out, so the daily windows stay on NDFD. Watches and warnings carry no weight at this range; if one is ever issued whose valid period genuinely reaches Day 2, it raises the level through the floor instead.

Day 3

Two days out, local calendar day
ERO 1.5 SPC 1.0 WPC QPF 1.0 NDFD QPF Daily 1.0

Same product mix as Day 2, with the NDFD daily rainfall weight stepped down slightly because forecast skill decreases with lead time. Watches and warnings act through the floor only, in the rare case one is valid that far ahead.

Rules That Apply to Every Window

Weighted blend. Each window's product scores are multiplied by the weights shown above and averaged, so no single soft signal can flip a quiet day on its own. The result maps back to the five threat categories.

Warnings are graded by distance from the basin. A Flash Flood or Flood Warning polygon that touches or overlaps a basin boundary forces that window to CRITICAL. A warning polygon within 5 miles of a boundary forces HIGH — close enough to matter, but not yet over the watershed. Beyond 5 miles a warning sets no floor at all and the other products stand on their own. Mill Creek and Castle Creek are tested independently, and the nearest-miss distance is recorded either way.

Watches set a floor too. An active Flood Watch or Flash Flood Watch forces the affected window to at least HIGH. Watches apply only to the windows their valid times actually cover — a watch for today does not raise the Day 2 or Day 3 outlook.

AFD language informs, but does not override. Plain-language signals from the Grand Junction forecast discussion (scores 1–2) feed the blend and the NWS Watch/Warn + AFD indicator, but only actual watch and warning products can force the overall level upward.

Outlooks score on distance, not just containment. The national ERO and SPC outlooks are drawn at a scale far larger than these watersheds, so an outlook edge a few miles away still says something about the day. Both products therefore score a nearby area at a reduced level rather than ignoring it — but SPC's General Thunderstorm and Marginal categories get no distance grace beyond 50 miles, since a broad low-end area two counties away is not information about Mill Creek.

“Corroborated cell” means more than one. The short-range HRRR forecast scores on either the basin average or a wet cell anywhere in the basin, so a concentrated storm core over the headwaters is not averaged away. To avoid a single stray model cell setting the tone, the trigger uses the second-wettest cell — a real storm core covers several 3 km cells, an artifact covers one.

Rainfall intensity can raise a level on its own. If the model forecasts an unusually wet single hour inside the window — 0.50" in an hour for Monitor, 0.75" for Elevated — that sets a minimum level even when the totals above stay low. Short, intense bursts are what drive flash flooding in these basins.

Observed radar scores the wettest cell, not the average. A Moab convective core routinely covers under 5% of a watershed, so a basin average would hide it. This is also why a CRITICAL radar tier can sit beside rain gauges reading 0.00 — with only a handful of gauges across roughly 500 km², the expected number that catch a core that small is well under one. The dashboard reports how many radar cells made up the core so the two readings can be reconciled at a glance.

Day 3 note. NDFD rainfall grids extend about 72 hours, so the Day 3 daily total can exclude the final early-morning hours of that day. Coverage is tracked and displayed with the data.
